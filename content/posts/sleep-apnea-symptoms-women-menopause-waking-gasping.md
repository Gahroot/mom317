Waking in the dark and wondering what just happened can feel lonely, especially when tomorrow is already on your mind. If you've been asking whether this is simply another thing to accept after menopause, I want you to have a more useful place to start: a clear question for your clinician.

## Could sleep apnea explain my nighttime waking?

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) describes sleep apnea as breathing that repeatedly stops and restarts during sleep.[^1] Obstructive sleep apnea, or OSA, happens when the upper airway repeatedly becomes blocked, reducing or stopping airflow.[^1]

The NHS lists frequent waking and gasping, snorting or choking noises during sleep among sleep apnea symptoms.[^5] Those are details I would put near the top of an appointment note, using the words that best describe what happened.

My aim here is to make that conversation less mysterious. You don't need to arrive with a diagnosis or know the right technical terms. You can begin with, “I'm waking at night, and I want to ask whether sleep apnea should be considered.”

## Why does menopause belong in this conversation?

Women may be at increased risk of sleep apnea during and after menopause.[^2]

In the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort Study, postmenopausal women had higher odds of sleep-disordered breathing than premenopausal women after adjustment for age, body habitus, smoking and other potential confounding factors.[^3]

I would read that as a reason to ask about evaluation, rather than a verdict about your own nights. The study compared women at different menopausal stages.[^3]

The study's authors recommended prioritizing evaluation for sleep-disordered breathing in menopausal women reporting snoring, daytime sleepiness or unsatisfactory sleep.[^3] If you have been searching for permission to bring up a persistent sleep problem, that is a useful starting point for the appointment.

## What if I don't sound like the stereotypical snorer?

NHLBI identifies insomnia, tiredness, morning headaches and frequent waking as symptoms women with sleep apnea report more often than men.[^2] Women with sleep apnea may not snore.[^2]

That is why I would avoid opening with, “I probably don't have it because nobody complains about my snoring.” Start with your actual experience instead.

You might say, “My main concern is being awake in the middle of the night,” or, “I'm getting through the day, but it feels like a struggle.” These are examples of language to borrow only if they fit. There is no need to make your story sound more dramatic, or more like somebody else's, to ask a straightforward question.

## What should I mention if I wake gasping?

NHLBI lists gasping for air and breathing that starts and stops during sleep as sleep apnea symptoms.[^4] You may be unaware of these nighttime symptoms until someone else tells you.[^4]

The NHS advises seeing a clinician for symptoms such as breathing pauses during sleep, gasping or choking noises, or persistent daytime tiredness.[^5]

For a conversation about waking gasping, I would write down what I remember without trying to explain it: “I woke suddenly and felt as though I was gasping.” If somebody heard or saw something, I would put their description beside mine, clearly marked as their observation.

If you sleep alone, I would simply tell the clinician that too. Don't invent an answer to “Do you snore?” An honest “I don't know” belongs in the conversation.

## What other details are worth writing down?

NHLBI also lists dry mouth, daytime sleepiness, fatigue and waking often to urinate among possible sleep apnea symptoms.[^4] I would mention whichever of those you actually notice, using the list as a starting point for conversation.

A sleep diary can track sleep duration, sleep quality and daytime sleepiness, and those details can inform a clinician's assessment.[^6]

My suggestion is a short, ordinary note rather than an elaborate spreadsheet. You could include:

- What you remember about the waking, in your own words.
- Whether someone observed breathing pauses or unusual noises.
- How you felt when you got up and during the following day.
- When you first noticed the pattern.
- The question you most want answered at the appointment.

I would leave unknown details blank rather than guess. I would also take the notes I already have to the appointment, even if they cover only a few nights. Think of them as a description of your experience, not homework you have to finish before making contact.

## How can I ask for screening or a sleep assessment?

A clinician evaluating sleep apnea will ask about symptoms, risk factors and family history.[^6]

I would open with one clear request: “Could we discuss whether I need an evaluation for sleep apnea?” Then I would describe the nighttime waking before moving on to the rest of my questions.

For persistent symptoms, talk with your clinician.[^4] A sentence you could adapt is: “This keeps happening, and I'd like us to make a plan for assessing it.”

If the conversation feels rushed, I would ask to leave with a specific next step: an assessment, a discussion about testing, or an explanation of what the clinician wants to consider first. Before leaving, I would repeat the plan back in my own words and ask whom to contact if I have questions. You deserve to understand the plan you are agreeing to.

## What happens during a sleep study?

Sleep studies can identify the type of sleep apnea and how severe it is.[^6] An overnight laboratory sleep study records brain waves, heart rate, breathing and blood oxygen levels.[^7]

Your doctor determines whether testing should take place at a sleep center or at home with a portable device.[^7]

Before booking, I would ask the testing service practical questions: Where do I go? What should I bring? What instructions should I follow? Who do I call if I cannot manage the equipment?

I would also mention any worries about the appointment itself, including privacy, cost or sleeping somewhere unfamiliar. Those are fair questions. Ask for the instructions in writing if that is your preference, and ask when and how you will hear the results. I would want the name of the person responsible for explaining them.

## Can a questionnaire or home test settle the question?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends against diagnosing OSA with questionnaires alone.[^8] After a screening discussion, I would ask what comes next.

AASM recommends a laboratory sleep study after a home sleep apnea test that is negative, inconclusive or technically inadequate.[^8]

For adults with severe insomnia, AASM recommends laboratory polysomnography rather than home sleep apnea testing.[^8]

AASM supports home testing with a technically adequate device for uncomplicated adults whose signs and symptoms indicate increased risk of moderate to severe OSA.[^8]

If a home test is proposed, I would ask, “Why is this the appropriate test for me?” I would also ask, “What will we do if the result doesn't answer the question?” Keep a copy of the report and bring your remaining questions to the follow-up conversation.

## What can I do with this information today?

You don't have to turn tonight into an investigation. My practical suggestion is to choose one next step: write down your main concern, contact your clinician, or prepare your questions for an appointment already booked.

I would put “Could sleep apnea be part of this?” at the top of the page, followed by what I actually know. Leave room for uncertainty. The purpose is to ask a clear question and get a clear plan, without having to figure everything out alone in the dark.
