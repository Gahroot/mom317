It's 3am, and you're awake again. I know the feeling of looking toward the clock and wondering whether to stay put or give up on the bed for a while. You deserve an answer that makes room for your actual night, including the damp pillowcase and tomorrow's responsibilities.

## Should I get out of bed if I can't sleep at night?

Usually, yes, if you remain awake: NHS guidance suggests getting up after around 20 minutes, doing something relaxing, and returning when sleepy.[^1] That's the practical answer, but I would read it with kindness rather than turn it into another instruction to perform perfectly.

Getting up can sound like a big undertaking when all you want is to stay under the covers. I would keep the idea small: a temporary pause, somewhere comfortable, with nothing to accomplish. You aren't being asked to start your day, clean the kitchen or earn your way back into bed.

For me, the useful question is, “What does this advice mean in a real bedroom?” Perhaps you share yours with a partner. Perhaps the only other seat is beside the bed. Perhaps you first need dry nightclothes. Those details deserve a place in the conversation.

## Why does the advice focus on the connection between bed and sleep?

Stimulus control aims to replace the association between bed and wakefulness, frustration or worry with an association between bed and sleep.[^3] “Bed equals sleep” is a plain-language way of describing that aim.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine conditionally recommends stimulus control on its own for adults with chronic insomnia disorder.[^2] The word “conditionally” matters when reading advice that can otherwise sound like a universal command.

I don't want you to hear the explanation as blame. There is no reason to turn a wakeful night into a case against yourself. If you've been lying there because you're exhausted and desperately want to sleep, that deserves understanding.

I would treat the advice as a plan to discuss and practise, rather than a verdict on what you've done wrong. You can be thoughtful about a bedtime habit without making yourself responsible for every difficult night. I'd leave the self-criticism out of the plan altogether.

## Do I need to watch the clock for exactly 20 minutes?

The AASM's earlier practical guideline describes approximately 20 minutes of perceived wakefulness and specifically advises against clock-watching.[^3] I would underline “approximately” before anything else.

My suggestion is to avoid setting a countdown or checking whether you've reached a precise minute. I wouldn't add another alarm to the bedside table. I would use the rough timing as a reference, rather than an appointment I must keep.

Imagine noticing, “I'm still awake, and I've been trying for a while.” That's the sort of ordinary observation I would bring to this advice. I wouldn't ask myself to reconstruct the exact moment I woke or subtract minutes from the time until morning.

And if you've already checked the clock several times, I wouldn't turn that into another failure. You can put the phone down and decide what to do next without reviewing the whole night.

## What could I actually do while I'm out of bed?

My suggestion is to choose the place and activity in daylight. I would pick a comfortable chair, put a familiar book nearby and decide in advance that this isn't the time for household jobs.

I'd make the choice deliberately unambitious. A few pages of a book I've already read would be my preference over an absorbing new mystery. Quiet music would be another option I'd consider. I wouldn't use the pause to answer messages, check the bank balance or finish tomorrow's presentation.

The AASM's practical instructions say to do a relaxing activity until drowsy, return to bed, and repeat if necessary.[^3]

I would avoid giving the activity a target: no required chapter, playlist length or completed task. You're allowed to put the book down halfway through a page. You're also allowed to dislike the first activity you chose and pick a different one the next evening.

If you share a room, my practical suggestion is to agree on the arrangements beforehand. Decide where the book lives and which chair you'll use. That conversation belongs in daylight, when neither of you is negotiating over a lamp at 3am.

## What if I woke because of night sweats?

The NHS says sleep problems during perimenopause and menopause may be worse with night sweats.[^4] That detail belongs in any conversation about getting out of bed in midlife.

If your clothes or bedding are wet, my suggestion is to deal with the wet things first. I wouldn't ask you to sit through a timed interval in damp nightclothes to prove you're following the instructions. I'd keep a spare top and pillowcase within easy reach, and choose bedding I can change without hunting through a cupboard.

For hot flushes and night sweats, NHS suggestions include lightweight clothing, a cool bedroom, a fan or a cold drink.[^5]

I would plan those practical arrangements around your preferences and your household. If a partner shares the bed, talk through what a middle-of-the-night change might look like. You might agree on where spare bedding goes or keep a separate light cover handy.

Changing a pillowcase and deciding whether to spend time in a chair are two separate decisions. I wouldn't treat every trip out of bed as a formal exercise. Sometimes the immediate task is simply, “I want something dry to wear.”

## Who should get individual advice before following this approach?

AASM notes that stimulus control may need safety adaptations for people at high risk of falls, with mobility issues, or using sedative-hypnotic medicines.[^2]

If that describes your situation, my suggestion is to ask your clinician for an adapted plan before trying a standard set of instructions. I'd be specific: “What should I do when I'm awake in bed, given my mobility and medicines?”

You don't have to apologise for the layout of your home or the limitations you're working with. I would describe the actual route, the available seating and any assistance you usually need. An instruction on a webpage doesn't know your bedroom.

I also wouldn't interpret this post as a reason to change a prescribed medicine. Bring the medicine's name and your questions to the conversation.

## When is it time to talk to a clinician?

The NHS advises seeing a GP when sleep trouble has lasted for months, habit changes haven't helped, or insomnia makes daily life hard to cope with.[^7]

For persistent symptoms, I would book the conversation rather than keep collecting more nighttime rules. My suggestion is to bring a short account in your own words: what happens, how often, what you notice when you wake and what the following day feels like.

The AASM strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I, for adults with chronic insomnia disorder.[^2] One question I'd take to an appointment is, “Would CBT-I be appropriate for me?”

The NHS also advises seeing a GP for regular night sweats that wake you or worry you, or night sweats with unexplained weight loss.[^6] I would mention sweating explicitly, even if the appointment was originally booked to discuss sleep.

## What would I put in a simple plan for tonight?

I'd write three personal reminders: choose somewhere comfortable to sit, set out dry nightclothes if wanted, and leave the chores until daylight. Nothing elaborate, and nothing to score in the morning.

I would also leave room for a clinician's individual instructions. My preference is for a plan that acknowledges the woman who has to use it: her home, her comfort, her responsibilities and her questions. You deserve that much care in the advice you receive.
