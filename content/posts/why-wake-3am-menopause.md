It's 3:17. You know because you looked. Again.

I spent 15 years waking up at about that time, so I know how that hour feels. The house is quiet, your mind isn't, and you start working out how many hours are left before the alarm. If you're in perimenopause or menopause and this is your night too, here is what the research actually says about why it happens. I'm sticking to what's been measured, not what sounds good on social media.

## Is waking in the night really a menopause thing?

Yes. It's one of the most common sleep complaints of this stage of life. A review of the research on sleep across the menopause transition found that self-reported sleep problems rise during the transition, and *night-time awakenings* in particular[^1]. It isn't trouble falling asleep at bedtime. It's waking up partway through the night.

The same review describes an interview study of almost 1,000 women. About a quarter of perimenopausal women (26%) met the criteria for insomnia, and difficulty *staying* asleep was the most common symptom[^1].

So if you've been wondering whether something is wrong with you, you're far from alone. This is a recognised, well-studied pattern.

## Do hot flushes and night sweats actually wake you up?

This is the best-documented cause. The NHS describes night sweats simply as hot flushes that happen at night, and notes that sleep problems may be worse if you have them[^3].

The most useful study I've found measured this directly instead of asking women to remember. Researchers recorded 34 perimenopausal women overnight in a sleep lab, using sensors on the chest that pick up the skin changes of a hot flush, alongside full sleep monitoring[^2]. They found:

- **About 69% of hot flushes came with an awakening**, within a few minutes of the flush starting[^2].
- On average, hot flush-related waking added up to **about 17 minutes a night**, roughly a quarter of all the time those women spent awake in the night[^2].
- The impact varied a lot from night to night and from woman to woman[^2].

That last point matters. Hot flushes explain a real part of night waking, but not all of it. The review authors put it this way: not every hot flush disturbs sleep, and not every awakening is caused by one, but the two are strongly linked[^1].

Sometimes you won't remember feeling hot at all. You just find yourself awake, maybe throwing off the covers, maybe damp at the neck. That can still be a night sweat.

## Why 3 a.m., and not midnight?

There's no 3 a.m. alarm built into a menopausal body. What the timing probably reflects is how sleep is put together across a night.

We get most of our deep sleep in the first half of the night. As the night goes on, the dream-heavy REM stages get longer, especially in the second half[^4]. Deep sleep is harder to wake from. So the early-morning hours are when sleep is lighter and a hot flush, a full bladder or a noise is more likely to tip you into being fully awake.

Then there's what happens once you're awake. Glance at the clock, start calculating, and the worry that follows makes it harder to drift back off. That's why we wrote a separate, practical guide on [what to do when you can't get back to sleep](/blog/cant-get-back-to-sleep-menopause).

## Is it cortisol, or blood sugar?

You'll see a lot of confident claims online that a cortisol spike or a blood-sugar dip is "the real reason" women wake at 3 a.m. Neither is among the main causes named in the research review I've relied on here. It points to hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and other sleep disorders as the main contributors to sleep problems in the menopause transition[^1].

That doesn't mean stress hormones play no part in any one woman's night. It means I'd be careful with anyone selling a single, simple cause, especially when they're selling something to go with it.

## What else can wake you at 3 a.m. in menopause?

Several other causes become more common at this stage, and more than one can be in play at once.

### Needing to pee

The NHS lists needing to pee more often, including at night, among the symptoms of perimenopause and menopause[^3]. If you wake, go to the bathroom and then lie awake, the bladder may be the trigger and the wakefulness the bigger problem.

### Low mood and anxiety

The risk of depression rises during the menopause transition, and mood and sleep affect each other in both directions[^1]. Poor sleep can lower your mood, and low mood can break up your sleep. If the 3 a.m. thoughts are heavy, or the days feel grey, that is worth raising with a doctor in its own right.

### Sleep apnoea, restless legs and limb movements

Sleep-disordered breathing (including sleep apnoea), restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder can all disturb sleep around and after menopause. Sleep-disordered breathing becomes more common in women after the transition[^1]. Loud snoring, someone noticing you stop breathing, waking with a gasp or a headache, or being very sleepy in the day are all reasons to ask about a sleep assessment.

### Everything else midlife brings

The usual suspects still count: a room that's too hot, noise, alcohol, caffeine, pain and stress are all common causes of insomnia[^7]. Midlife often stacks several of them on top of hormonal change.

## Does it go away after menopause?

For many women night waking eases over time, but it doesn't follow a neat timetable. The NHS notes menopause symptoms usually last for several years, sometimes longer, and can change along the way[^3]. Sleep problems can also outlast the hot flushes that started them, partly because months of broken nights build habits and worries of their own.

This is the main reason I don't think "just wait it out" is good advice. If it's been going on for a while, it's worth treating as its own problem.

## What can actually help?

The right answer depends on what's driving your waking, which is why a conversation with a clinician is so useful. Here are the evidence-based routes to know about:

- **Treating hot flushes and night sweats.** The Menopause Society's 2023 position statement says hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment for hot flushes and night sweats, and lists recommended non-hormone options, including cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), clinical hypnosis and several prescription medicines, for women who can't or don't want to take hormones[^6]. Which one suits you is a decision to make with your doctor.
- **Treating the insomnia itself.** CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) is a structured programme that targets the waking-and-worrying cycle directly. We cover it, and what to do in the moment, in [our guide to getting back to sleep](/blog/cant-get-back-to-sleep-menopause).
- **Checking for other causes.** Sleep apnoea, bladder problems, low mood and restless legs each have their own treatments, and none of them will fix themselves just because the hot flushes do.

## When should you see a doctor?

The NHS suggests seeing a GP about insomnia if changing your sleep habits hasn't helped, if you've had trouble sleeping for months, or if it's affecting your daily life in a way that makes it hard to cope[^7].

For night sweats specifically, the NHS advises seeing a GP if they regularly wake you or worry you, or if they come with a very high temperature, feeling shivery, a cough or diarrhoea, or weight loss you can't explain[^5].

It helps to go in with a week or two of notes: roughly when you woke, whether you were hot, whether you got up to pee, how long you were awake and how you felt the next day. That turns "I sleep badly" into something a clinician can work with.

### What should I write down?

Keep it simple enough to do half-asleep. A notepad by the bed works better than a phone, which tempts you to start scrolling. For each night, note:

- roughly what time you went to bed and woke up (don't check the clock in the night; an estimate in the morning is fine),
- what you noticed on waking: hot or sweaty, needing the loo, a racing mind, or nothing at all,
- roughly how long you were awake,
- anything unusual that day: alcohol, a late meal, a stressful event.

After a week or two, patterns often show up that you'd never spot night by night. If most wakings start with feeling hot, for example, that points the conversation towards treating hot flushes.

## The short version

Waking at 3 a.m. in menopause is common, it's real, and it isn't a failure of willpower. Hot flushes and night sweats are the best-documented cause. Lighter sleep later in the night makes that hour an easy one to wake in. A bladder, mood, anxiety or a sleep disorder can add to it or stand in for it. Treatments with real evidence behind them exist, and you deserve a proper conversation about them.
