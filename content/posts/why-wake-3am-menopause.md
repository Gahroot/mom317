It's 3:17. You know because you looked. Again. I know how lonely that hour can feel: the quiet house, the counting, the thought of tomorrow. If this is your night, I want to give you a careful answer, including where the evidence stops.

## Is waking in the night really a menopause thing?

The menopause transition is associated with more insomnia symptoms, especially difficulty staying asleep.[^1]

That distinction matters to me. When I say “a bad night”, I want the conversation to include the hours after bedtime, too. “I got to sleep, then spent a long stretch awake” describes something different from “I couldn't fall asleep.”

You do not need to choose a medical label before describing your own nights. I would start with what actually happened and leave room for questions. There is no need to make your experience sound more dramatic, or more tidy, to deserve a conversation.

## Do hot flushes and night sweats actually wake you up?

The NHS says sleep problems may be worse if you also have night sweats.[^3]

A sleep-laboratory study recorded sleep and hot flushes in 34 perimenopausal women.[^2] In that study, 69.4% of objectively recorded hot flushes were associated with an awakening.[^2]

I find that useful because it gives us a measured connection. I would keep the words “associated with” when explaining it to a friend. I would not turn a finding from that group into a diagnosis for her.

If you notice heat or sweating, I would write that down in your own words. If you do not, I would write that down too. There is no need to fill in a symptom because an article mentioned it.

## Why 3 a.m., and not midnight?

The evidence described above does not give me an answer to that exact clock-time question. I would not stretch it into one.

For me, the useful starting point is the sequence: when did I go to bed, roughly when did I wake, and what did I notice? “Three in the morning” tells one part of the story. I would want to bring the rest of the night into the conversation as well.

I also would not turn remembering the time into another task. If you would rather leave the clock alone and estimate in the morning, that is how I would approach these notes. They are a rough account, not a test.

## Is it cortisol, or blood sugar?

I would not label your waking a cortisol spike or a blood-sugar dip based on the hour alone. Those are questions I would take to a clinician, rather than conclusions I would borrow from an online post.

My rule as a reader is to ask: did the study actually measure the thing the headline names? Who took part? Does the finding describe an association, or establish a cause?

Those questions do not make a difficult night less frustrating. They are simply the standard I want this blog to meet. When an explanation goes beyond the evidence I can verify, I would rather leave the question open than hand you certainty I do not have.

## What else can wake you at 3 a.m. in menopause?

I would bring more than the time on the clock to an appointment. Here are a few things I would make space to discuss.

### Needing to pee

The NHS lists needing to pee more often, including at night, among urinary symptoms that can occur during perimenopause and menopause.[^3]

In my notes, I would distinguish “I woke needing the loo” from “I was already awake and decided to go.” I would report whichever describes the night, without deciding for myself what came first medically.

### Low mood and anxiety

The NHS lists stress, anxiety, depression, noise, bedroom temperature, alcohol and caffeine among common causes of insomnia.[^7]

If mood is part of what you want to discuss, I would put it on the appointment list in plain language. You do not have to squeeze everything into a question about hormones.

### Other sleep problems

The NHS also lists restless legs syndrome among conditions that can cause insomnia, and interrupted breathing during sleep among things that disrupt sleep.[^7]

I would mention anything a partner has noticed, alongside what I remember myself. I would leave the interpretation to the clinician rather than use a checklist to diagnose myself.

## Does it go away after menopause?

The NHS says menopause symptoms usually last several years, sometimes longer, and can change over time.[^3]

I would not read that general statement as a countdown for your particular sleep problem. It does not give me a date to promise you.

I also would not make “wait and see” my only plan if I wanted an appointment now. You can ask about what you are experiencing today without first predicting how long it will last. I would want that conversation to end with a clear next step and an understanding of when to check back.

## What can actually help?

Here are two different questions I would bring to a clinician: “What are my options for hot flushes?” and “What are my options for the time I spend awake?”

### What options are recommended for hot flushes?

The Menopause Society's 2023 position statement identifies hormone therapy as the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms.[^6]

That statement recommends nonhormone options for vasomotor symptoms, including cognitive behavioural therapy, clinical hypnosis and certain prescription medicines.[^6]

I would ask which options are appropriate for me, why, and what I should understand before choosing. I would also ask what the proposed treatment is intended to address, so we are talking about the same goal.

### What about the insomnia itself?

The AASM's 2021 guideline strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) for adults with chronic insomnia disorder.[^4]

That is a specific recommendation I would take into the appointment. I would ask whether an assessment for chronic insomnia is appropriate and, if so, how to access CBT-I locally or remotely. I would want a name, a referral route or a clear next step to follow.

## When should you see a doctor?

The NHS advises seeing a GP if sleep-habit changes have not helped, problems have lasted months, or insomnia makes daily life hard to cope with.[^7]

For night sweats, the NHS advises seeing a GP if they regularly wake or worry you, or occur with a very high temperature, feeling hot, cold or shivery, a cough, diarrhoea, or unexplained weight loss.[^5]

I would use those recommendations as a reason to book, rather than a reason to keep proving I can manage alone. If the appointment feels daunting, I would write one opening sentence beforehand: “I'm waking in the night, and I want to talk about it.”

### What should I write down?

My suggestion is a brief morning note, using whichever format you prefer:

- Rough bedtime and waking time.
- What you noticed when you woke.
- Roughly how long you remember being awake.
- What you want the clinician to know about the following day.
- Your most pressing question.

I would leave gaps where I do not remember. I would also bring a list of medicines and anything else I take, without changing them on the strength of this article.

## What should I take from this?

I cannot give you a personal diagnosis from a familiar hour on the clock. What I can offer is a starting point: describe your nights, keep the questions specific, and ask a clinician about persistent symptoms. You deserve a conversation in which your own experience is heard.
