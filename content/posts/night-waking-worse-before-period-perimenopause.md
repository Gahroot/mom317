If you've started dreading the nights before your period, I understand why you want an explanation. Being awake is hard enough without wondering why it seems to happen on the same stretch of the calendar. I'd take that observation seriously, while leaving room for questions.

## Is there evidence that sleep changes before a period in midlife?

A study of 163 late-reproductive-age women used wrist actigraphy to measure sleep across a menstrual cycle or 35 days, whichever was shorter.[^2] Sleep efficiency declined across the cycle, with a more pronounced decline in the premenstrual fourth week.[^2]

I read that as a reason to bring your calendar into the conversation. I would be more cautious about using it to predict next Tuesday, or to explain one particular awakening.

For your own notes, I'd distinguish waking briefly from lying awake wishing you could return to sleep. You don't have to turn either experience into a technical score.

## What did the small early-perimenopause study actually find?

A 2015 laboratory study examined 20 women in early menopausal transition on one night in each of the follicular and luteal phases.[^1] Both women with insomnia and those without it had more awakenings per hour in the luteal phase, when progesterone was raised.[^1]

With so few participants and sampled nights, I'd read this as evidence of an association, rather than an explanation for every reader's nights. A comparison between two sampled nights also leaves me wanting to know what happened on the nights in between.

## How should I describe the timing?

The menstrual cycle can be divided into follicular and luteal phases.[^8] A cycle's length is counted from the first day of menstrual bleeding to the start of the next period.[^8]

You don't need to memorize the vocabulary to describe your experience. I'd use ordinary words: “The worst nights seemed to come before bleeding,” or “I haven't noticed a consistent connection.”

For the question in this article, I'd also keep the two research findings distinct: a comparison of cycle phases and an analysis of premenstrual timing. I wouldn't turn them into a claim that every woman has a predictable bad-sleep week.

## Does a pattern prove that a particular hormone is waking me?

I wouldn't make that leap. “I notice more waking before my period” describes an observation. Naming a particular hormone as the cause asks much more of that observation.

My approach would be to bring the timing to a clinician as an open question. You might ask: “Could my cycle be relevant, and what else should we consider?” That wording leaves room to investigate without dismissing what you've noticed.

I'd apply the same caution to a tidy explanation found online. Before accepting it, I'd ask whether the evidence concerns women in perimenopause, whether sleep was measured, and how many nights were included. Those are questions about the evidence, not a test you have to pass.

## Could PMS or night sweats belong in the conversation too?

The NHS lists trouble sleeping among symptoms of premenstrual syndrome, or PMS, which occur in the weeks before a period.[^3] PMS symptoms can differ between women and vary from month to month.[^3]

If you've noticed mood changes or discomfort alongside the waking, I'd mention those observations together. I wouldn't try to give the pattern a diagnosis myself. You can simply describe what happened and when.

The NHS also says sleep problems during perimenopause and menopause may be worse with night sweats.[^4] If you remember waking hot, damp, or needing to change your clothes, I'd put that in your notes. If you don't, I'd leave it blank rather than assuming sweating must have happened.

I want the record to sound like your actual night, with room for “I don't know.” There is no need to fill every gap with a theory.

## What if my periods are no longer predictable?

During perimenopause, periods may happen more often or less often, and bleeding may become heavier or lighter.[^4]

For that reason, my personal suggestion is to write down actual bleeding dates rather than build your notes around an expected date. When bleeding starts, you can look back at the nights before it.

I'd record the quieter nights too. Otherwise, you're collecting only the nights that prompted the question. A blank space, “slept as usual,” or “nothing memorable” is enough for my purposes; this doesn't need to become a second job.

If the dates don't form the pattern you expected, bring that uncertainty along. You don't need to make the calendar tell a neat story before asking for a conversation.

## What should I record alongside the awakenings?

NHLBI says a sleep diary kept for one to two weeks before an appointment can help a doctor understand sleep problems.[^5] Its suggested information includes sleep and wake times, naps, daytime sleepiness, and the timing of caffeine, alcohol, and exercise.[^5]

For a possible premenstrual pattern, I'd add one small piece to whatever sleep record you already use: bleeding dates. I'd keep these questions nearby:

- Was there bleeding today, or did it begin after this night?
- Did I remember brief awakenings, a long awake stretch, or both?
- Did I notice sweating, discomfort, or another detail I want to mention?
- What would I most want my clinician to understand about the following day?

These are my suggested prompts, rather than a diagnostic checklist. I'd use plain descriptions and leave uncertain details uncertain. “Awake for a while” is an acceptable note if that's all you remember.

For PMS symptoms, the NHS suggests keeping a diary for at least two menstrual cycles and taking it to a GP appointment.[^3] I'd use that as something to discuss, without postponing an appointment just to finish a record.

The useful addition here is the timing beside the sleep notes. You don't need a separate tracking system, a new device, or a page of calculations.

## How can I bring this up without sounding certain about the cause?

I'd start with the experience and the question:

> “I've noticed a possible connection between my awakenings and the days before bleeding. Here are the dates I wrote down. Can we look at the pattern together?”

Then I'd say what is hardest: the repeated waking, the long wait to fall asleep again, or getting through the next day. Choose your own words. This is your account of your nights.

If the appointment is short, I'd circle the main concern on the page before going in. I'd also write down the question I don't want to forget. My preference would be one clear conversation starter over an elaborate summary that takes the whole appointment to explain.

## When should I talk to a clinician about persistent waking?

The NHS advises seeing a GP if sleep trouble has lasted for months or affects daily life enough to make coping difficult.[^6] Please talk to a clinician about persistent symptoms, even while you're still working out whether there's a cycle pattern.[^6]

For adults with chronic insomnia disorder, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I.[^7] The same guideline conditionally advises against using sleep hygiene alone for chronic insomnia disorder in adults.[^7]

You could ask, “Does my sleep problem need assessment for insomnia, and would CBT-I be appropriate?” I'd bring the cycle question and the sleep question to the same appointment. You deserve space to discuss both, without having to arrive with the answer already worked out.
