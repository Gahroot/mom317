If you're reading this, there's a good chance you're awake right now, or you were last night, staring at a clock that says 3:07 and wondering why this keeps happening. I've been there. The house is quiet, your mind is not, and the thought of tomorrow makes it worse. First, you are not imagining it, and you are not alone. Sleep trouble is common as women move through the menopausal transition.[^1]

This post is a hub. It gives a plain-language overview of the main factors linked to waking in the night during perimenopause. Other posts on this blog go deeper on individual topics. None of this is a diagnosis. It's a map to help you have a better conversation with a clinician.

## Why do I keep waking up at 3am during perimenopause?

The honest answer is that there usually isn't one cause. Researchers who review sleep in the menopausal transition describe a mix of hormonal, physical and psychological factors, and these often overlap.[^1]

I also want to be upfront about something. The research I found talks about waking during the night in general, not about 3am specifically. I couldn't find good evidence that 3am is a special hour. Part of why it feels like "always 3am" may simply be that it's when many of us notice the clock. A repeating pattern is still worth taking seriously, because it may point to a repeating trigger.

Here are the main factors that come up.

## Do changing hormones affect sleep in perimenopause?

Perimenopause is the stretch of time when your ovaries are winding down, and hormone levels, especially estrogen and progesterone, don't decline in a neat straight line. They fluctuate.[^1] Researchers think these shifts may be one piece of why sleep changes during this time, although the exact mechanisms are still being worked out.[^1]

In other words, if your sleep fell apart while your cycles were changing, that timing is worth noting. It doesn't prove hormones are the only cause, but it's a useful clue to share with your clinician.

## Can hot flashes and night sweats wake me up?

Yes, they can. The NHS lists hot flashes and night sweats among common menopausal symptoms, and notes that they can disturb sleep.[^2] Reviews of sleep in the menopausal transition also link these vasomotor symptoms with sleep disruption and waking in the night.[^1]

You may not always remember the heat itself. Some women describe waking with a damp pillow, throwing off the covers, or just surfacing wide awake with no clear reason. If that sounds familiar, it may be worth jotting down for a week or two whether you felt warm, whether you were sweating, and what time it happened.

### What if I'm not having obvious hot flashes?

That's possible too, and it's one reason it helps to look at the whole picture. Sleep can be disturbed by things other than hot flashes, including the factors below.

## Could stress and anxiety be part of it?

Very possibly. The NHS lists stress and anxiety among common causes of insomnia.[^6] Menopause itself can bring anxiety, low mood and trouble sleeping, and the NHS lists these together as common symptoms.[^2]

Midlife is also often a time when a lot lands on us at once: work, teenagers, ageing parents, our own changing bodies. A quiet 3am can be when all of that finally gets the floor. It can become a loop, where worrying about being awake makes it harder to drift off again.

If this sounds like you, you might ask a clinician about cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, which the NHS describes as an evidence-based approach.[^6] The Menopause Society's nonhormone position statement also reviews cognitive behavioral therapy and clinical hypnosis among the non-hormone approaches it discusses for menopausal vasomotor symptoms.

## Could it be sleep apnea?

This is the one many women have never been told about. Sleep apnea, where breathing repeatedly pauses or becomes shallow during sleep, is often thought of as a problem for men. But in the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort Study, postmenopausal women had higher odds of sleep-disordered breathing than premenopausal women, and that held after accounting for age and body mass index.

The NHS describes the signs of sleep apnoea as including loud snoring, gasping or choking during sleep, and feeling very tired during the day.[^5] Women don't always notice these, and a partner might. Waking suddenly and feeling unrested are things I'd mention to a clinician whatever the cause, but if you also snore or have been told you stop breathing at night, please raise it. Sleep apnea can be assessed, and it's much better to know than to guess.

## Does alcohol make waking at night worse?

I'm not here to lecture anyone about a glass of wine, but this one is worth knowing. A widely cited review of alcohol and sleep found that alcohol may shorten the time it takes to fall asleep, but it is associated with disrupted sleep, particularly in the second half of the night.

That can look a lot like "I fall asleep fine, then I'm wide awake in the early hours." If that's your pattern, it may be worth noticing, without judgment, whether the waking follows evenings when you've had a drink. It's one of the few factors on this list that's easy to observe for yourself.

## Why does it happen every night, not just sometimes?

There are a few reasons an occasional bad night can turn into a nightly pattern, and I'll say upfront that these are possibilities, not diagnoses.

- **Several factors can stack.** A little hormonal change, a little stress and an evening drink might each be minor on their own. Together they may add up.[^1]
- **Worry about sleep can become its own problem.** The NHS notes that stress and anxiety are common contributors to insomnia, and dreading the night can feed into that.[^6]
- **A condition may be going unrecognised.** Sleep-disordered breathing, for example, becomes more common after menopause.

This is also why a sleep diary can be useful. When I was working out my own pattern, I found it helpful to write down what time I woke, whether I felt hot, what I'd had to drink, and how stressed the day had been. It's not scientific, but it gives a clinician something concrete to work with.

## When should I talk to a clinician about waking in the night?

I'd encourage you to book an appointment if:

- The waking has been going on for weeks, or it's affecting your daytime life, mood or safety, for example if you're drowsy when driving.[^6]
- You snore loudly, gasp or choke in your sleep, or someone has noticed you stop breathing.[^5]
- Hot flashes or night sweats are regularly disturbing your sleep.[^2]
- You're feeling persistently anxious or low.[^2]

You don't have to wait until it's "bad enough." There are evidence-reviewed options for menopausal symptoms, and a clinician can talk through which ones fit your health history. If you feel brushed off, it's fine to say clearly, "My sleep has changed and it's affecting my life. Can we look into why?"

## What can I take from all this?

Waking in the night during perimenopause is common, and it usually has more than one contributor. Hormone shifts, hot flashes and night sweats, stress, sleep-disordered breathing and alcohol are all part of the conversation.[^1][^2][^6] The 3am part may be less magical than it feels, but a pattern is still a clue.

If you take one thing away, let it be this: you're not failing at sleep. Your body is going through a big transition, and it's reasonable to ask for help working out what's going on.
