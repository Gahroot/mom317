It's 3:17 a.m. and you're awake again. The sheets are damp, your heart is thumping, and you're throwing off the covers only to pull them back ten minutes later when the chill hits. If this is your nightly routine, I want you to know two things: you are far from alone, and there is more than one thing worth discussing with a clinician. I've been in that bed, and the internet didn't make it easier. Most of what I found was either a one-line tip or a sales pitch. So here is a calm, balanced walk through the options clinicians actually talk about.

## Why do night sweats keep waking me up?

Night sweats are essentially hot flashes that happen while you sleep. They belong to a group of symptoms called vasomotor symptoms, which are common during the menopause transition.[^4] When they strike at night, they can break up your sleep, and the research literature links vasomotor symptoms with sleep disruption in midlife women.[^4]

It's worth knowing that night sweats and poor sleep don't always share a single cause. Other conditions, such as sleep apnea, can also disturb sleep in midlife.[^4] Some medicines, infections and thyroid problems can cause sweating too. So if your night sweats are new, severe, or come with fever or unexplained weight loss, please see a clinician rather than assuming it's menopause.

## What bedroom and bedding changes are worth trying?

These are the things most of us try first, and they're low-risk. None of them is a treatment, and I don't want to oversell them, but they can make a rough night a little more manageable:

- **Keep the room cool and well ventilated.** A fan or an open window is simple to try.
- **Choose breathable layers.** Many women prefer natural fibers or moisture-wicking fabrics, and layering lets you shed covers quickly without fully waking.
- **Keep a change of nightwear and a towel within reach.** Damp sheets and clothes are often what make it hard to settle again.
- **Keep water by the bed.**

Here is the honest part. The Menopause Society's 2023 position statement on non-hormonal management did not find enough good-quality evidence to recommend several lifestyle approaches, including cooling techniques, trigger avoidance, exercise or yoga, as treatments for vasomotor symptoms. That doesn't mean you shouldn't make your room comfortable. It means these are comfort measures, not a fix, and it's okay if they only take the edge off.

## Do triggers matter, and should I keep a diary?

Many women notice that certain things seem to set off a sweat: alcohol in the evening, spicy food, a warm room, stress. Trigger awareness is a common suggestion, and a few weeks of notes can show you your own pattern. As above, the evidence for avoiding triggers as a treatment is limited, and triggers differ a lot from person to person. Think of the diary as information rather than a rule book.

A diary has another benefit: it gives your clinician something concrete. Jot down how many nights a week you wake, roughly how many times, how long it takes to settle, and how tired you are the next day.

## What about hormone therapy for night sweats?

This is the option most people have heard of. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is a treatment used to relieve menopause symptoms such as hot flushes and night sweats.[^7] In the UK, NICE's menopause guideline advises clinicians to offer HRT for vasomotor symptoms after discussing the short- and long-term benefits and risks.[^6]

It isn't right for everyone, though, and this is where individual assessment really matters:

- Women with a uterus who use estrogen are offered combined HRT, meaning estrogen plus a progestogen.[^6]
- The risks linked to HRT, such as blood clots and breast cancer, differ depending on the type of HRT and how it is taken.[^8]
- Your own medical history, including any personal or family history of these conditions, needs to be part of the discussion.[^8]

No blog post can tell you whether hormone therapy suits you. A clinician who knows your history can. If your clinician has dismissed the question without a proper conversation, it's reasonable to ask for a longer appointment or a second opinion, ideally from someone with menopause training.

## What non-hormonal prescription options exist?

Some women can't take hormones, and others simply prefer not to. The Menopause Society's 2023 position statement reviewed the evidence on non-hormonal approaches and recommends several options, including cognitive behavioral therapy, clinical hypnosis, certain antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs) and gabapentin.

A few things to keep in mind:

- These are prescription medicines (or, in the case of CBT and hypnosis, clinician-guided therapies), so they involve a conversation, not a shopping basket.
- They can have side effects, and the right choice depends on your other health conditions and medicines.
- If you're already taking an antidepressant or another medicine, don't stop or change anything without talking to your prescriber.

Options and availability keep changing, so ask your clinician what is currently suitable for you.

## Can CBT help with night sweats and sleep?

Cognitive behavioral therapy is on the Menopause Society's list of recommended non-hormonal approaches for vasomotor symptoms. I want to be precise about what it is. CBT isn't a way of talking yourself out of a hot flash. It's a structured, clinician-guided approach to how you respond to symptoms and to the sleeplessness that can follow.

If the hardest part of your night is lying awake dreading the next wake-up, it may be worth asking your clinician whether a sleep-focused form of CBT is available where you live, and what it would involve.

In the UK, NICE's menopause guideline encourages clinicians to give individualized information and to discuss the benefits and risks of the available options.[^6] If you're interested in CBT, ask your clinician about trained therapists or programs near you.

## What about supplements and "natural" remedies?

I get asked this constantly, and I understand why. When you're exhausted, a bottle with a promising label is tempting. But the Menopause Society's 2023 statement did not find enough good-quality evidence to recommend over-the-counter supplements for vasomotor symptoms. Products also vary in what they contain, and some can interact with medicines. If you're thinking about trying one, please run it past a pharmacist or clinician first. Nothing on this page, and nothing I'm building at 3:17, should be read as a fix for night sweats.

## How do I decide what's right for me?

There's no single right answer, and that's the point. Here's how I'd approach it:

1. **Start with comfort measures** that cost little and carry little risk: a cooler room, breathable layers, a spare set of nightwear.
2. **Track your nights for two or three weeks.** Note sweats, wake-ups and how you feel the next day.
3. **Book a visit with a clinician** if nights are regularly broken, if your daytime life is suffering, or if anything about the sweats is new or worrying.
4. **Ask about the full menu**: hormone therapy, non-hormonal prescriptions, and CBT or other therapies. Ask what each would mean for your health history, and what the downsides are.
5. **Revisit the plan.** What suits you now may change, and it's fine to adjust.

Persistent symptoms deserve a proper conversation, not just endurance. You don't have to earn help by suffering for longer.
