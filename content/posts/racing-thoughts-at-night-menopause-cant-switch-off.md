It is the middle of the night, and your mind has opened every unfinished conversation, tomorrow's list and next year's worries. I understand the longing behind “Why can't I switch my brain off?” I want to meet that question with kindness and clear evidence, without making another wakeful night something you have failed at.

## Why does my mind feel so switched on?

Researchers describe hyperarousal as relatively increased arousal in physiological, brain, and cognitive-emotional systems.[^2] A 2023 review describes evidence for cognitive-emotional hyperarousal in insomnia, including sleep-related thoughts and worries.[^2]

Perhaps you call it nighttime rumination; perhaps you simply say, “My brain won't stop.” Your version might sound like this: “I should be asleep by now. How many hours are left? What if tomorrow is impossible?” I would start by noticing the words, without demanding an immediate answer to every one.

My suggestion is to try a small change in wording: “I'm having the thought that tomorrow will be impossible.” You can leave that sentence there. You do not owe your midnight thoughts a detailed argument, a life plan or a cheerful replacement thought.

## Is this menopause, anxiety or something else?

A review of sleep during the menopausal transition discusses links between poor sleep, hot flashes and depression.[^1] Some sleep complaints may coexist with depressive disorders or be attributable to sleep-related breathing or movement disorders.[^1]

I would bring the whole picture to an appointment. What woke you? What were you thinking about afterward? Were you hot, uncomfortable or worried about something particular? How did the following day feel?

For my own notes, I'd use ordinary language: “awake worrying about work,” “woke feeling hot,” or “felt low most of the afternoon.” I would keep the entries brief enough to bring along and read aloud. There is no need to arrive with a polished explanation.

## How do mood symptoms fit with wakeful nights?

The NHS lists being unable to relax, worrying about the past or future, and difficulty sleeping among possible symptoms of anxiety.[^7] Low mood can also involve tiredness and problems sleeping, according to the NHS.[^8]

I would want both sleep and mood on the agenda, with room to talk about each. If a conversation starts and ends with “that's menopause,” I would ask to keep going: “Can we also talk about how worried or low I've been feeling?”

You deserve time to describe what is happening in your own words. I would include what matters during the day, too: the meetings you dread, the plans you cancel, or the enjoyment you miss. Bring whichever examples are true for you.

## What is CBT-I, and what could I ask about?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I, for adults with chronic insomnia disorder.[^3]

NHLBI describes cognitive therapy within CBT-I as addressing nervousness about being unable to sleep.[^6] AASM also conditionally recommends against using sleep hygiene alone as treatment for chronic insomnia in adults.[^3]

My opening question would be straightforward: “Could we discuss CBT-I and whether it is appropriate for me?” I would also ask who provides it, what sessions involve, and how to find an affordable option.

I'd take along one recurring thought, such as “If tonight is bad, tomorrow is ruined,” and ask how the approach would address it. That gives the conversation something concrete to start with. You do not have to know the terminology before asking for an explanation.

## Is there guidance specifically for menopause?

For people aged 40 or over with sleep problems associated with hot flushes or sweats, NICE says to consider menopause-specific CBT.[^4] NICE includes this option alongside other management choices, for people for whom other options are contraindicated, or for those who prefer not to try them.[^4]

I would ask which approach a clinician is proposing and why: “Are we discussing CBT for insomnia, menopause-specific CBT, or a plan that includes both?” I would want the explanation in everyday language.

Then I would ask about practicalities: individual or group sessions, appointments from home, and what I would be asked to do between sessions. My preference would be a plan I understand well enough to explain back, with a clear opportunity to ask questions.

## What would getting out of bed have to do with it?

NHLBI describes stimulus control as going to bed only when sleepy, getting out of bed if you cannot sleep, and reserving bed for sleep and sexual activity.[^6]

Its description of sleep restriction involves a specific amount of time in bed that can increase as sleep improves.[^6]

I would discuss the time-in-bed part with a clinician and ask for an individual plan. I'd want clear instructions about what to do and when to check back.

For the getting-up part, my practical preference would be to decide in advance where I could sit comfortably and what quiet book I might pick up. I'd keep that choice simple and account for mobility, stairs and anyone else sharing the space. You can ask how to adapt the instructions to your home.

## Could scheduled worry time give my concerns a place?

The NHS suggests setting aside 10 to 15 minutes to write down worries and explore solutions.[^5] It also recommends distinguishing problems you can act on from hypothetical worries beyond your control.[^5]

I would try this earlier in the evening, with a notebook and a definite stopping point. My page might have three headings: “What's on my mind?”, “Is there a next step?” and “When will I do it?”

For example, I might write: “I'm worried about the bill. Tomorrow, after breakfast, I'll check the balance and make the call.” For something beyond my control, I might simply write: “I have no action to take on this tonight.”

The NHS suggests reminding yourself that a worry can wait for your designated worry time.[^5] I would use a plain phrase: “I've written that down; I'll return to it tomorrow.”

## What relaxation approach could I try?

The AASM conditionally suggests relaxation therapy as a single-component treatment for adults with chronic insomnia disorder.[^3] NHS worry guidance includes breathing, mindfulness and meditation among strategies for calming down and returning attention to the present.[^5]

My preference would be to choose one short guided exercise from that NHS page and listen to it in the daytime first. I'd want to know whether I like the voice, the pace and the instructions before choosing it in the middle of the night.

For an unguided moment, I might sit comfortably, notice the contact of my feet with the floor and let a few ordinary breaths come and go. I'd treat that as something gentle to try. If I disliked it, I would stop; there is no assignment to complete.

## When should I talk to a clinician?

The NHS advises seeing a GP if anxiety is hard to cope with or the things you are trying yourself are not helping.[^7] For low mood, it advises seeing a GP if it lasts more than two weeks, feels hard to cope with, or self-help is not helping.[^8]

For persistent nighttime waking, I would also arrange a conversation with a clinician. I would bring my brief notes and say plainly: “I'm waking at night, I can't settle, and this is affecting my life.”

Before leaving, I would ask what the next step is and when we will review it. If low mood or anxiety remains part of the picture, I would keep saying so. You do not have to make your concerns smaller to fit the appointment.
