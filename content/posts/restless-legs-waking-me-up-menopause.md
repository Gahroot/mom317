If you're awake again and your legs won't let you settle, I understand wanting a name for what's happening. At that hour, even deciding how to describe it can feel like too much. Let's take the question one piece at a time, without asking you to diagnose yourself in the dark.

## Could restless legs explain why I can't settle again?

Restless legs syndrome can make it difficult to return to sleep after waking.[^2] Its characteristic pattern includes an urge to move, symptoms that worsen at rest and in the evening or night, and temporary relief with activity.[^8]

The part I'd pay attention to is the sequence: what you were doing, what you felt, and what happened next. When you're thinking, “restless legs waking me up—menopause?”, start with those details rather than the label.

For your own notes, I would ask:

- What did I feel before I moved?
- Was I lying down, sitting, or already walking?
- Did I want to move, or did movement happen without my choosing it?
- What changed when I moved, and when I stopped?

Write whatever fits your experience. “I don't know” belongs on the page, too. You don't have to make your night match a checklist.

## Does being in menopause mean I have restless legs syndrome?

A Canadian population-based study of women aged 45 to 60 found no association between menopausal status and restless legs syndrome.[^5]

That finding is a reason for me to be careful with the word “menopause” in this conversation. I would bring my menopause history to the appointment, but I would also want time to describe the legs themselves.

The updated international RLS diagnostic criteria require consideration of other conditions that can produce similar symptoms.[^4]

I wouldn't want you to leave this article feeling you must decide between “it's menopause” and “it's restless legs.” A more useful opening for the appointment might be: “I'm in this stage of menopause, and here's what happens when I lie down.” Let your clinician take the assessment from there.

## How is this different from a leg cramp?

A leg cramp happens when a leg muscle tightens and causes sudden pain that can make movement difficult.[^3] The calf is a common location, and the muscle can remain sore after the cramp ends.[^3]

I'd describe a sudden painful tightening in those words, rather than automatically calling it restlessness. Equally, if “tightening” doesn't fit what you feel, leave that word out. Your own description matters more than borrowing a medical term.

The NHS notes that leg cramps can resemble restless legs syndrome.[^1]

For the appointment, consider writing down where the discomfort was, whether you felt a muscle tighten, and whether soreness remained afterward. If you experience different sensations on different nights, give each its own line. I would resist squeezing every experience into one description just to make the story tidier.

## What if my partner says I kick in my sleep?

Periodic limb movements of sleep are involuntary twitching or jerking movements during sleep.[^2] Many people with RLS have these movements, but most people with these movements do not have RLS.[^2]

I'd bring your partner's observations to the appointment as observations: what they saw, whether you seemed asleep, and how often they noticed it. Ask them to use ordinary words rather than choose a diagnosis.

Keep a separate note of what you remember feeling while awake. You could make two columns headed “what I felt” and “what someone else saw.” If the second column is empty because you sleep alone, leave it empty. Please don't turn this into another task you have to perform perfectly before asking for care.

## What should I bring to a clinician when symptoms disrupt sleep?

The NHS advises seeing a GP when restless legs symptoms stop you sleeping, affect your mental health, or persist despite self-care.[^1]

I'd make the first sentence of the appointment about the disruption: “This is keeping me awake, and I want it assessed.” You don't need a confident guess about the diagnosis to say that.

My suggested notes would include:

- The words you'd use for the feeling: use your own language.
- Whether it began before bed, after waking, or at another time.
- Whether you were still or moving when it began.
- What you noticed after changing position or getting up.
- Whether the same thing happened on other nights.
- What you want the clinician to understand about the following day.

If keeping notes feels burdensome, I would bring one example instead. Choose a night you remember, tell the story in order, and say which parts are uncertain. I would also bring a list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, and supplements, along with questions about anything recently started or changed.

## Why might my clinician discuss iron or medicines?

The AASM's 2025 guideline recommends regular iron studies, including ferritin and transferrin saturation, for patients with clinically significant RLS.[^6] Its good practice statement is based on expert consensus.[^6]

I would frame the question as, “Are iron studies appropriate for my situation?” Leave decisions about supplementation to your clinician after assessment. This article isn't a recommendation to start iron, choose a dose, or buy a supplement.

The same guideline identifies alcohol, caffeine, certain medicines, and untreated obstructive sleep apnea as factors to address in RLS management.[^6]

Bring questions about your existing medicines to the prescriber rather than changing them on your own. My preference would be to leave with a written plan: what is being assessed, what happens next, and whom to contact if I have questions. Ask for clarification if any part of that plan is unclear.

## Should I ask about sleep apnea as well?

Sleep apnea involves breathing that stops and starts during sleep.[^7] Symptoms can include loud snoring, gasping or choking noises, and repeated waking.[^7]

For a separate discussion, read [our existing sleep-apnea article]({{sleep_apnea_article_url}}). Bring any breathing observations to your appointment alongside your description of the leg symptoms.

The NHS advises seeing a GP for breathing pauses during sleep, gasping or choking noises during sleep, or persistent daytime tiredness.[^7]

I would put those details on their own line in my appointment notes. That gives you room to ask about breathing as well as leg sensations, without having to decide in advance which question deserves all the attention.

## What can I do with this information tonight?

My suggestion is a small one: put a notebook nearby and give yourself permission to write only a few words. You could note the time, the sensation, and what you chose to do next. Save the longer account for daylight if that feels easier.

I wouldn't make tonight a test of whether you can follow every sleep tip you've ever read. Choose one practical step, such as putting your appointment questions somewhere you'll find them tomorrow. Ask a trusted person to sit with you while you write them if you'd like company.

You deserve a conversation that takes your description seriously. You don't have to arrive with the right medical vocabulary, a perfect diary, or a theory about your hormones. Start with what you noticed and what you need to ask. That's enough to open the conversation.
