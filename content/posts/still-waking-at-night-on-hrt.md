If you're awake again after starting prescribed HRT, I can understand the frustration. You made a decision about your care, and now you're looking at the clock, wondering what comes next. I want to give that question the attention it deserves, without asking you to diagnose yourself in the dark.

## Why am I still waking at night on HRT?

There are two questions I would take to an appointment: how your menopause symptoms have changed, and what your nights look like now. Put both on the agenda, even if you originally booked a straightforward prescription review.

The NHS says HRT can take up to three months to work fully.[^1] It also says to speak to your doctor if you do not feel better after three months.[^1]

Meanwhile, the NHS lists stress, anxiety, caffeine, alcohol and some medicines among the possible causes of insomnia.[^4] That is a reason to ask for a broad conversation about your sleep, rather than arrive with a conclusion about your prescription.

For me, the useful starting point is a description: “I'm still waking, and this is what happens.” You do not need to turn that description into a diagnosis before asking for an appointment.

## What does the research actually say about HRT and sleep?

A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis found improved self-reported sleep quality with menopausal hormone therapy in women who had hot flushes or night sweats at baseline.[^2]

The same review found no difference in sleep quality in the subgroup without those symptoms.[^2] Differences between the studies limited the authors' certainty about the evidence.[^2]

I would keep the wording “self-reported sleep quality” in mind when reading promises about sleeping through the night. In your appointment, ask about your own experience: are you waking hot, lying awake without feeling hot, or noticing something else?

You do not have to choose between saying “some things have changed” and “my nights are still difficult.” Bring both observations. I would want the conversation to leave room for the whole picture, including anything you are pleased about and anything you are still struggling with.

## What should I bring to my HRT review?

The NHS says a review is usually recommended three months after starting HRT or changing its type, followed by yearly reviews if everything is OK.[^3]

If symptoms have not improved or side effects are a problem, a GP may suggest changing the dose, changing the type of HRT or referral to a menopause specialist.[^3] Leave decisions about prescription changes to your prescriber; do not change your treatment independently.

I would bring a short list rather than rely on remembering everything in the room:

- Your prescription names, when you started them and how you currently take or use them.
- What you hoped to discuss when HRT was first prescribed.
- What has changed since then, and what has stayed the same.
- Questions about anything you have found difficult, including following the prescribed routine.
- Your most pressing concern about the nights you are having now.

These are my suggestions for organising the conversation, not a test you need to pass. A few notes on your phone or a piece of paper are enough. You can also ask your clinician to write down the agreed next steps and when you should get back in touch.

## Which other sleep problems should I ask about?

I would describe the details before naming a condition. “I wake several times” and “my partner says I gasp” are different observations, and both belong in the conversation if they apply to you.

### What if someone has noticed snoring or gasping?

The NHS lists loud snoring, gasping or choking noises, breathing that stops and starts, and repeated awakenings as symptoms of sleep apnoea.[^5] It advises seeing a GP for symptoms such as breathing pauses, gasping or persistent daytime tiredness.[^5]

If someone has noticed something while you sleep, I would write down their words rather than try to interpret them. Ask your clinician whether an assessment is appropriate. You do not need to come with a recording or a diagnosis to raise the question.

### What if my legs feel uncomfortable at night?

Restless legs syndrome can involve a strong urge to move the legs and uncomfortable sensations such as tingling or itching.[^6] Symptoms are usually worse when resting at night and can affect sleep.[^6]

The NHS advises seeing a GP if these symptoms are stopping you sleeping.[^6] I would describe what the sensation feels like, when you notice it and what you do in response. Let the clinician decide what questions or investigations belong next.

### What if I am simply awake for long stretches?

Regularly waking during the night, lying awake or waking early and being unable to return to sleep are among the NHS-listed symptoms of insomnia.[^4]

Ask directly: “Should we assess my sleep as well as review my menopause symptoms?” You are allowed to ask that without deciding which explanation is right.

## Is there a treatment conversation beyond reviewing my HRT?

For adults with chronic insomnia disorder, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends multicomponent cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, often called CBT-I.[^7]

The same guideline suggests that clinicians should not use sleep hygiene alone as treatment for chronic insomnia disorder.[^7]

Those recommendations give you something specific to ask about: “Would CBT-I be appropriate for me, and how would I access it?” Ask who would assess you, what the programme involves and who to contact if you have questions.

I would also ask your clinician to explain how any proposed sleep care fits alongside your existing prescriptions. Write down the plan in plain language. There is no need to leave the appointment pretending you understand an unfamiliar term; ask for it to be explained again.

## How can I describe my nights without making a project of it?

The NIH's National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute suggests keeping a sleep diary for one to two weeks before an appointment.[^8] Its suggested entries include sleep and wake times, naps, daytime sleepiness, caffeine, alcohol and exercise.[^8]

My suggestion is to keep the format simple. Use a notebook or a plain note, and write a short account in the morning. Include anything you especially want to discuss, such as feeling hot when you woke or what someone else noticed.

I would separate observation from interpretation. “Awake, felt hot, changed my top” tells the story without needing to say why it happened. “Awake, no obvious trigger” is also something you can put on the page.

Take the notes you have, even if there are gaps. I would not delay an appointment just to produce a tidier diary. The point is to bring your experience into the room, in your own words.

## When should I contact my clinician?

The NHS advises seeing a GP when insomnia makes daily life hard to cope with or when sleep problems have lasted for months.[^4]

If your symptoms are persistent, contact your clinician. In the message, I would say that you are taking prescribed HRT, describe the awakenings and explain what you want reviewed. Mention any observations about breathing or uncomfortable legs if those apply.

Before the conversation ends, I would ask three things: “What are we reviewing first? What should I do next? When should we speak again?” You deserve a clear next step, even if the explanation is not settled at the first visit.
