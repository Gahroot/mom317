Being awake in the dark with an intimate discomfort you do not quite know how to describe can feel lonely. I know how long the middle of the night can feel. You deserve to be heard without having to find the perfect words first.

## Could the itching or soreness be connected with my waking?

A review of vulval pruritus, or vulval itching, reports that it can affect sleep.[^2] That is a reason to bring the discomfort into the conversation about your nights.

It is also the limit of what I would claim here. The review concerns vulval itching broadly.[^2] I would not use that finding to decide why you woke on a particular night. I would describe what happened in the order you remember it: “I woke and noticed soreness,” or “I felt an itch and then became fully awake.” Either account is worth sharing, even if you cannot tell which came first.

This article focuses on soreness, dryness and itching. If your main experience is waking to use the bathroom, I would make a separate note of that for your appointment. You do not need to fit every nighttime experience into this one explanation.

## What does menopause have to do with vaginal dryness?

The NHS lists menopause as a possible cause of vaginal dryness, and soreness or itching among its symptoms.[^1]

You may hear the phrase “genitourinary syndrome of menopause,” shortened to GSM. The North American Menopause Society's 2020 position statement discusses GSM and includes symptomatic vulvovaginal atrophy within it.[^6] There is no need to arrive at an appointment already knowing whether that label applies to you.

I would start with ordinary language. “Dry,” “sore,” “itchy,” “raw-feeling,” and “uncomfortable” are words you can use if they match your experience. If you are unsure, say that too. You are giving an account of what you feel, rather than choosing a diagnosis from a list.

I would also make room for the part that matters most to you: “This is happening when I'm trying to sleep.” There is no requirement to begin with sex, or to discuss the discomfort only in that context.

## What if the discomfort is on the outside?

The vulva is the area around the opening to the vagina.[^4] If that is where you notice the discomfort, use the word “outside” when describing it.

The NHS describes lichen sclerosus as a skin condition that causes itchy white patches, often on the genitals.[^4] It recommends seeing a GP for an itchy white patch on the genitals or skin.[^4]

I include that example to underline the value of an assessment. You do not need to compare yourself with photographs online or decide whether a particular patch matches a particular condition before making an appointment.

A note such as “The itching feels on the skin outside, on the left,” is enough to start with. So is “I cannot tell exactly where it is.” I would rather bring an imperfect description to a clinician than wait until I could name everything confidently.

## Could it be something other than menopausal dryness?

The review of vulval itching discusses contact dermatitis, lichen sclerosus and infectious vulvovaginitis among associated conditions.[^2] The NHS vaginitis guidance advises against self-diagnosis.[^3]

That is why I would avoid declaring, “It's definitely menopause,” on the strength of the time of night or my age. I would bring the question instead: “Could this be dryness, or does something else need checking?”

The NHS advises assessment for first-time vaginitis symptoms, unusual discharge, or symptoms that differ from usual or persist.[^3]

You can mention whatever you have already tried without feeling that you must defend the decision. I would take the packaging, or a photograph of the label, so I did not have to remember a product name under pressure. An honest account is more useful to this conversation than a polished one.

## What can I do without turning bedtime into another project?

The NHS advises against perfumed washes, douches and creams or moisturisers not intended for vaginal use.[^1]

My practical suggestion is to put any product questions on paper for a pharmacist or clinician. I would ask, “Is this intended for the area where I am uncomfortable?” and “What should I avoid while we work out what is going on?” There is no need to build a complicated bedside routine from internet suggestions.

If I were preparing for an appointment, I would keep a small notebook nearby and use it only when I wanted to record something. My suggested prompts would be:

- What did I notice when I woke?
- Where did it feel uncomfortable?
- Was this familiar or different?
- What had I used on the area?
- What do I most want to ask?

These are conversation prompts, not a scoring system. A few words are enough. I would not set alarms, check repeatedly, or turn the night into an investigation. If writing at night feels like another demand, make a brief note in the morning instead.

You can also write down the sentence you want to lead with. Mine would be: “I'm waking at night and noticing intimate discomfort, and I'd like it assessed.”

## When should I arrange a clinical review?

The NHS advises GP review for vaginal dryness affecting daily life, or lasting a few weeks despite self-care.[^1]

If this is a persistent part of your nights, I would bring it up plainly. You do not have to make the appointment sound more dramatic than your experience, or minimise it with “It's probably nothing.”

Vaginal bleeding after menopause should be checked by a GP even if it happens only once or is a small amount.[^5] The NHS says postmenopausal bleeding is not usually serious, but can be a sign of cancer.[^5]

At a vaginitis assessment, a clinician may examine the skin and vagina and take an infection swab.[^3] You can ask for an uncomfortable examination to stop at any point.[^3]

My suggestion is to take your questions with you and ask for explanations as you go. You can say, “Please tell me what you're checking,” or “Could we pause while I ask something?” You deserve a conversation you can follow.

If I felt tongue-tied, I would hand over a short written summary: “I have noticed this discomfort for ____. It feels like ____. I notice it when ____. My main question is ____.” Leave any blank empty if you do not know the answer. You can also write down what you would like explained before you leave. There is no test to pass here; this is your account of your nights, in your own words.

## What might a clinician discuss, and what should I expect about sleep?

The 2020 Menopause Society statement says the choice of therapy for GSM depends on symptom severity, the safety and effectiveness of options for the individual, and patient preference.[^6] That puts your questions and preferences within the discussion.

The NHS says vaginal oestrogen is used for menopause-related vaginal dryness and irritation, but not for insomnia.[^7] Keep that distinction in mind when discussing what you want from care.

I would ask: “What are we addressing with this option?” “How will we review my discomfort?” and “When should I come back if I'm still waking?” Ask for the plan in words you can use at home, including who to contact with further questions.

Before leaving, I would check that I could describe the next step back to the clinician. If I still had a question, I would say it aloud: “I understand the plan for the discomfort. Can we also agree what happens next with my waking?” I would put any agreed review date in my calendar and keep my notes together for that conversation.

My hope is that you leave with a clearer next step and permission to speak plainly about an uncomfortable subject. You do not need to settle the cause alone in the dark.
