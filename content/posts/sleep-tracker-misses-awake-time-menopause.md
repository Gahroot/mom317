It is a lonely feeling: you remember being awake in the dark, then your app greets you with a cheerful sleep report. I don't want you to have to argue with a screen before you can talk about your night. Let's put the report beside your experience and look at what each can tell you.

## Why does my tracker count time I remember being awake?

In a laboratory study of seven consumer sleep-tracking devices, the devices detected sleep more reliably than wakefulness.[^2] The study involved healthy young adults, rather than a menopause-specific sample.[^2]

That gives me a reason to be cautious about a reassuring graph. It doesn't give me grounds to declare your particular device wrong, or to calculate how many minutes it missed.

A review of wearable sleep technology describes devices collecting activity and other signals, including heart rate, to derive information about sleep.[^3] I would read that output as an estimate, with questions attached: Which device produced it? What exactly is the app reporting? What evidence applies to this model?

Perhaps you remember lying very still, thinking about tomorrow. I wouldn't try to reconstruct your device's algorithm from that memory. I would write down both observations: “I remember being awake” and “the app labelled this interval sleep.” I'd keep those statements side by side for the conversation.

## Does menopause make sleep trackers less accurate?

I would want evidence that specifically addressed women at this stage of life before answering that question. I also wouldn't turn a disagreement with your own tracker into a claim about every woman in menopause.

The NHS notes that difficulty getting to sleep or staying asleep can affect daily life, and that sleep problems may be worse alongside night sweats.[^6]

I would describe the night in my own words. If you remember waking hot, write that down. If you remember waking without feeling hot, write that down too. I would avoid filling in a cause just because menopause is part of the conversation.

When reading a device claim, my questions would be specific: Were women at my stage of life included? Was wakefulness assessed separately from sleep? Is the evidence about this device, or a different one? I wouldn't make answering those questions a condition of booking an appointment.

## Should I trust my memory or my sleep score?

I wouldn't ask you to pick a winner before you've spoken to someone. Your recollection is the account you can offer; the report is something you can bring alongside it.

NHLBI says clinicians may ask how often you wake at night and how long it takes to fall back asleep.[^5]

I would prepare a plain account without demanding a perfectly timed reconstruction. Try saying what you remember, what you estimated, and what you don't know.

Here is wording you could adapt: “I remember a long stretch awake after midnight. I didn't check the time. The app shows little wakefulness. The following morning, I struggled with my usual tasks.”

I would leave the uncertainty in that account. “About an hour” and “I'm not sure” are perfectly reasonable things to write on a page. There is no need to turn a rough recollection into an exact number just to match the app's precision.

If the app says “excellent” and that word feels nothing like your night, I would copy the word into the notes rather than argue with it. Then I'd add my own description underneath. I would want both accounts visible at the appointment.

## How is a sleep diary different from a tracker?

The Consensus Sleep Diary was developed through collaboration between insomnia experts and potential users as a standardized approach to sleep self-monitoring.[^4]

For your own notes, I would label each entry clearly: remembered, estimated, or copied from the app. I'd use those labels to keep the origin of each detail visible.

NHLBI suggests keeping a sleep diary for one to two weeks before seeing your doctor.[^5] It advises recording sleep and wake times, naps, daytime sleepiness, caffeine or alcohol use, and exercise.[^5]

### A simple way I would organize the page

I'd use a notebook, with space for:

- **My account:** What I remember about the night, with approximate times only where I have them.
- **My uncertainty:** Anything I cannot estimate or do not remember clearly.
- **The device report:** The date and a screenshot, kept separately from my account.
- **My next day:** A few words about how the day actually went.
- **My questions:** What I want to discuss rather than decide for myself.

I would write my account before opening the app, simply to keep my own words separate from its labels.

I would also resist rewriting yesterday's entry to make it agree with a new report. If I wanted to add something, I'd mark it as a later note. The aim is an honest record, not a tidy set of matching numbers.

## What should I bring when the report and my experience disagree?

In its 2018 position statement, the AASM said consumer sleep technology should not be used to diagnose sleep disorders.[^1] It also recognized a possible role for these technologies in patient-clinician conversations within an appropriate clinical evaluation.[^1]

I would bring a small, readable selection rather than an entire archive. My suggested appointment folder would contain:

1. **A short opening statement:** “My concern is repeated waking and how I feel afterward.”
2. **Your diary:** Keep your own descriptions visible, even where the app disagrees.
3. **A few dated screenshots:** Choose examples you particularly want to discuss.
4. **The device details:** Model, app name, and any settings or updates you know about.
5. **A written question:** “What would you assess given this pattern?”

The wearable-technology review identifies proprietary algorithms, device malfunction and firmware updates as factors to consider when using these devices in research or clinical settings.[^3]

I would also bring a medication and supplement list and a page of questions. My preference is to lead with the concern I want addressed, then show the supporting notes.

For example: “I'd like us to discuss the waking first. I have screenshots if you'd like to see them.” That's the order I would choose, especially if I were worried about spending the whole appointment explaining an app.

## Will I need a sleep study?

NHLBI describes sleep studies as tests that look for other sleep problems, including sleep apnea.[^5]

I would ask: “What question would a test answer in my case?” I'd want to hear the clinician's reasoning rather than make that decision from a graph.

You can also ask what the next step is if testing isn't suggested, and when to return for review. I would write that plan down before leaving.

If I didn't understand the explanation, I'd ask for it again in ordinary language. My notes would have three spaces: what we're considering, what happens next, and whom to contact with questions. I would leave room for the clinician's answers rather than fill those spaces in advance.

## When should I seek assessment despite a reassuring score?

The NHS advises seeing a GP if sleeping difficulties have lasted for months or are making daily life hard to cope with.[^8]

If symptoms persist, talk to a clinician.[^8] I would not wait for the app to produce a sufficiently bad score before booking that conversation.

You might begin with: “The numbers look reassuring, but this is still a problem for me. I'd like to discuss the waking and its effect on my day.” You don't have to arrive with a conclusion about the device. Bring the disagreement, your questions, and your account of the nights you are living through.
